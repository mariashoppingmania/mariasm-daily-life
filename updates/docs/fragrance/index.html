01 · START HERE

Your own scent story starts with curiosity.

No need to memorise every ingredient or follow the latest trend. Learn a few fundamentals, smell with patience, and discover what genuinely brings you joy.

01

Know the family

Fresh citrus, soft musk, lush florals or warm woods: a family gives you a useful starting point, not a rigid rule.

02

Follow the evolution

What you smell in the first minute can differ from the aroma after an hour. Allow the fragrance time to settle.

03

Sample before buying

Try one fragrance on each wrist, revisit it later and compare with your own notes. Save the full bottle for a favourite.

02 · THE SCENT WARDROBE

Twelve ways to feel something.

Fragrance families overlap and naming systems vary. Think of them as a mood board for the nose, not boxes every perfume must fit inside.

🍋

Citrus

Typical notes

Bergamot, lemon, grapefruit, mandarin

Sparkling, uplifting and often short-lived; delightful for hot weather or fresh layering.

🌸

Floral

Typical notes

Rose, jasmine, peony, iris, tuberose

From sheer petals to opulent white flowers. The largest spectrum is not automatically the sweetest.

🍑

Fruity

Typical notes

Peach, pear, berries, plum

Juicy and bright; commonly created through accords rather than extracting the aroma from the fruit.

🌿

Green

Typical notes

Violet leaf, galbanum, grass, fig leaf

Crisp, leafy, sometimes bitter; evokes crushed stems and new growth.

🌱

Aromatic

Typical notes

Lavender, rosemary, sage, thyme

Herbal, bracing and often fresh; popular in modern gender-free wardrobes.

🪵

Woody

Typical notes

Cedar, vetiver, sandalwood, patchouli

Dry, creamy, smoky or earthy; provides structure and warmth.

✨

Amber & spicy

Typical notes

Benzoin, labdanum, cardamom, cinnamon

Radiant, enveloping, resinous or spicy; a broad modern term, not one fixed ingredient.

🍮

Gourmand

Typical notes

Vanilla, praline, cacao, coffee, caramel

Delicious impressions that range from airy sweetness to rich dessert-like warmth.

🌊

Aquatic

Typical notes

Marine accords, sea salt, ozonic notes

Airy, watery or mineral; often built with modern aroma molecules.

🖤

Leather & smoke

Typical notes

Leather accords, birch tar effects, incense

Textural, smoky, dark or softly suede-like. More niche-leaning but not only for evenings.

🍃

Chypre

Typical notes

Citrus, floral heart, mossy/patchouli base

Traditionally contrasts sparkling top notes with earthy depths; modern versions reinterpret the structure.

🤍

Musk & skin

Typical notes

White musks, ambrette effects, clean woods

Close-to-skin, soft or laundry-clean; ‘musk’ on a note list does not necessarily mean animal-derived musk.

Remember: chypre and fougère also describe historic compositional structures. ‘Amber’, ‘oriental’, ‘clean’ and ‘skin scent’ are shifting marketing and olfactory terms; there is no single universal family chart.

03 · HOW A SCENT UNFOLDS

Top. Heart. Base.

Perfumers often describe a composition as a pyramid. Top notes introduce it, the heart gives it character, and base notes tend to be more persistent. But real formulas are blended systems: transitions overlap, and the pyramid is an educational model, not a stopwatch.

TOPCitrus · herbs
HEARTRose · jasmine · spices
BASEWoods · vanilla · resins · musks

A useful guide to perfume concentration

LabelCommonly quoted rangeWhat it may feel like
Eau fraîche~1–3%Very light mist, often casual and fleeting.
Eau de cologne~2–5%Traditionally fresh, aromatic or citrus-forward.
Eau de toilette~5–15%Often lighter in style, but not invariably weaker.
Eau de parfum~15–20%Often a fuller composition with more pronounced heart/base.
Parfum / extrait~20–40%Usually richer and more concentrated; projection may be softer, not louder.

Important: the percentages above are approximate industry conventions, not compulsory legal thresholds. The same perfume name in EDT and EDP can represent two different formulas. Longevity and projection depend on ingredients, skin, climate and application — concentration alone does not guarantee either.

04 · THROUGH TIME AND ACROSS CULTURES

A world history of fragrance.

Perfume has never belonged to only one capital, one tradition or one gender. This world tour highlights diverse histories without pretending that every region followed the same timeline.

FROM SMOKE TO SKIN

A history carried by trade, art and memory.

The English word perfume is associated with the Latin expression per fumum — ‘through smoke’. It hints at ancient incense and aromatic burning, but today's global fragrance culture includes oils, scented woods, waters, distillations and modern compositions.

Interpretations of some ancient recipes are debated; selected examples below are educational introductions rather than definitive histories of entire civilisations.

CHAPTER 01 · c. 3000 BCE · Ancient Egypt & Mesopotamia

Resin, ritual and oil

In Egypt and Mesopotamia, fragrant smoke, incense, ointments and scented oils carried religious and social significance. Frankincense, myrrh and aromatic woods moved across trading networks. Recipes and surviving vessels illuminate practices, but the exact smell of many ancient mixtures is uncertain.

Explore the aroma: Resins · oils · incense

CHAPTER 02 · Classical antiquity · Greece & Rome

The perfumed body

Greek and Roman cultures used scented oils in bathing, grooming and social life; perfumed spaces and objects also mattered. Aromatic imports linked Mediterranean consumers to trade routes reaching Arabia, Africa and Asia. Different social classes had very different access.

Explore the aroma: Rose · herbs · scented oils

CHAPTER 03 · South Asia · Ancient to modern

Attar, flowers and sandalwood

Across South Asia, flowers, woods, infused oils and incense appear in long-established aromatic traditions. Attar-making later became especially associated with distilling botanicals into an oil base; in places such as Kannauj, artisans still work with traditional apparatus. Practices differ across regions and communities.

Explore the aroma: Rose · vetiver · sandalwood

CHAPTER 04 · China & Japan · Historical traditions

The art of appreciating incense

In China, fragrant woods, incense and scented objects had ceremonial, literary and domestic uses. Japan developed kōdō, an art of appreciating fragrant woods, distinct from contemporary skin perfume. These practices involve cultural knowledge, not merely an ‘Asian scent style’.

Explore the aroma: Agarwood · sandalwood · incense

CHAPTER 05 · Middle East & North Africa · Medieval to present

Distillation and the scented household

Scholars and artisans across the Islamic world advanced and documented distillation, perfume recipes and aromatic materials. Rosewater, musk accords, bakhoor, amber-like accords and oud remain influential, though recipes and customs vary widely across countries.

Explore the aroma: Rosewater · oud · spices

CHAPTER 06 · Medieval & Renaissance Europe

Trade, scented waters and gloves

With increased commerce in aromatic materials and techniques, scented waters and perfumed accessories gained attention among European elites. Italian merchants and workshops played an important role; later French glove-making and perfumery became closely connected.

Explore the aroma: Citrus · herbs · gloves

CHAPTER 07 · 17th–18th centuries · Europe

Courts and eau de cologne

Perfume was a marker of status in some European courts, while early eau de cologne became known for its bright citrus and herbal character. Cologne is a fragrance type and tradition—not simply a synonym for any perfume marketed to men.

Explore the aroma: Bergamot · neroli · rosemary

CHAPTER 08 · 19th century · Grasse and new chemistry

From flowers to modern formulas

Extraction methods, production techniques and synthetic aroma molecules expanded the perfumer’s palette. The appearance of molecules such as coumarin and vanillin helped create effects that could not be achieved by distilling flowers alone.

Explore the aroma: Violet · tonka · new molecules

CHAPTER 09 · 1900–1950 · Couture and abstraction

A fragrance can be a fashion statement

Iconic launches including Guerlain’s Jicky (1889), Chanel No. 5 (1921) and mid-century compositions linked perfumery with fashion and creative abstraction. A fragrance no longer needed to smell like one literal flower.

Explore the aroma: Aldehydes · chypre · florals

CHAPTER 10 · 1950–1990 · International perfume culture

Everyday scent and new genres

Mass retail and department stores made fragrance part of everyday grooming for a wider audience. Green, aromatic fougère, oriental/amber and bold floral compositions flourished; marketing categories shaped expectations but never defined who may wear a scent.

Explore the aroma: Green · fougère · amber

CHAPTER 11 · 1990–2010 · Global design and niche

Aquatics, minimalism and gourmand

Clean musks, marine accords and transparent fragrances gained popularity, while gourmand scents embraced edible impressions like caramel and vanilla. Alongside major brands, independent perfumery offered experimental compositions.

Explore the aroma: Marine · musk · vanilla

CHAPTER 12 · 2010–2026 · A global conversation

Personal taste over gender rules

Niche houses, affordable Middle Eastern brands, perfume creators and online fragrance communities have expanded the ways people discover scent. Gourmand, skin-scent, oud, clean and unusual woody accords coexist. Sustainability and ingredient transparency are increasingly part of informed shopping.

Explore the aroma: Discovery sets · skin scents · oud

05 · INSIDE THE PERFUMER'S PALETTE

Ingredients & accords explained.

Some perfume notes are literal botanical materials. Others are artistic recreations made from several molecules. A note list is a description of a smell, not a full ingredient disclosure.

Bergamot

A fragrant citrus peel associated with sparkling openings; expressed bergamot oils can require controlled use for phototoxicity.

Rose

May be represented by rose absolute, rose oil or a composed rose accord; variations range from dewy to jammy.

Jasmine

Indolic, creamy or luminous aspects; formulas may combine natural extracts with aroma materials.

Tuberose

Lush and intoxicating, sometimes green or creamy. Its intensity is about style, not bottle concentration alone.

Iris / orris

Orris root yields precious materials used for a soft violet-like, buttery, cosmetic character.

Vanilla

Vanillin may come from natural sources or synthesis; a vanilla note is not necessarily vanilla extract.

Patchouli

An aromatic plant leaf oil with earthy, woody and chocolate-like facets depending on treatment.

Vetiver

From the plant’s roots; can smell earthy, dry, smoky, nutty or green.

Sandalwood

Natural sandalwood sources are limited and species matter; contemporary sandalwood effects may use aroma molecules.

Oud / agarwood

A complex fragrant wood material; commercial ‘oud’ fragrances often use constructed accords rather than natural oud oil.

Frankincense

Boswellia resin used as incense for centuries, with a bright, sometimes lemony-smoky scent profile.

Ambrox / ambergris accord

Modern ambergris-style effects can be recreated without using animal-origin ambergris.

Aldehydes

A wide chemical family; some convey soapy, waxy, citrus-peel or bright diffusion.

Iso E Super-style woods

A modern woody ingredient family often perceived as velvety, airy and subtle.

Calone-type marine notes

Associated with airy sea-breeze impressions. This is a constructed marine effect, not extracted seawater.

THE PERFUMER'S ART

Nature meets imagination.

Modern perfumery blends natural extracts with synthetic aroma materials. Neither is automatically better or safer; quality depends on formulation, safe use and personal preference.

08 · PRACTICAL FRAGRANCE RITUALS

Choose, wear, care, repeat.

A fragrance wardrobe works best when it reflects the wearer’s life, climate and comfort rather than a list of fashionable bottles.

A

Build a 3-scent wardrobe

Pick one gentle everyday fragrance, one bright warm-weather option and one expressive evening scent. A small collection you actually wear is enough.

B

Test without rushing

Start with blotters, narrow to two on skin, and revisit at 20 minutes and several hours. Note comfort, projection, dry-down and whether you still want to smell it.

C

Think about the setting

Close offices, trains and shared spaces reward restraint. Choose fewer sprays rather than assuming expensive perfume deserves a larger cloud.

D

Layer with care

Use compatible unscented moisturiser to reduce skin dryness. When combining fragrances, try one spray each; strong layering can overpower notes instead of enriching them.

E

Store away from heat

Keep bottles upright, tightly closed and away from sunlight and humidity. The bathroom shelf may look beautiful, but fluctuating temperature is unhelpful.

F

Buy the right size

Calculate price per millilitre, but choose a smaller bottle if you rarely wear a scent. A full bottle kept unused is never the best deal.

FRAGRANCE & OCCASION

What fits your day?

Work & shared spaces: soft musk, gentle citrus and clean woods with low projection.

Summer & seaside: citrus, green tea, transparent florals and marine accords.

Cosy evenings: amber, warm spice, cocoa, sandalwood and vanilla.

Celebrations: an expressive floral, chypre or personal signature — sprayed thoughtfully.

Gifts: travel sizes, discovery sets or a gift card when preferences are unknown.

09 · WHAT EVERYONE ASKS

Myths, answers & little truths.

A growing perfume collection should mean more pleasure, not more confusion. Open any question to explore the answer.

Do more expensive perfumes always smell better?

No. Materials, creative direction, packaging, production and branding all affect price. A fragrance that smells wonderful to you is better value than an expensive one you rarely wear.

Is an eau de parfum always stronger than an eau de toilette?

Not necessarily. Concentration bands are conventions rather than binding legal percentages, and formulations can differ. Judge on skin, not the abbreviation.

Can perfume expire?

Yes. Heat, sunlight and repeated exposure to air can change a fragrance. Store the bottle closed in a cool, dark area and check for major colour or odour changes.

Where should I spray it?

Start with one or two sprays on skin that tolerates fragrance, away from eyes and irritated areas. Test clothing only after checking staining risk. Avoid rubbing wrists together vigorously.

Do top, heart and base notes appear at exact times?

No. The note pyramid is a storytelling tool. Materials evaporate and mingle at different rates; you may notice facets overlapping.

Is ‘natural’ automatically safer?

No. Botanical oils and synthetic aroma ingredients can both cause reactions. Read the product label, use as directed, and stop using a product that irritates you.

How do I choose a perfume as a gift?

Find out which scent families the recipient already likes. A discovery set or gift card often beats blind-buying a large bottle.

How do I avoid counterfeit bottles?

Use official brand shops or reputable authorised retailers. Compare bottle, batch details and seal carefully; prices that are dramatically below market deserve scrutiny.

Are perfumes for women and men chemically different categories?

There is no olfactory rule requiring gendered choices. Wear what smells good to you: rose, vetiver, vanilla and woods are available to everyone.

What if my skin is sensitive to fragrance?

Avoid applying perfume to irritated skin. Follow ingredient and allergen labels; seek advice from a healthcare professional for persistent irritation or known fragrance allergy.
