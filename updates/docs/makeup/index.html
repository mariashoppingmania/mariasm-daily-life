03 / CULTURES, CENTURIES AND CHANGING IDEALS

The world history of makeup.

Makeup did not originate in one country. Ritual, trade, class, performance, cinema and technology have shaped different beauty practices worldwide. Historical materials and techniques should not be copied without safety checks.

THE LONG STORY OF PIGMENT

Beauty has always been an evolving language.

From ancient cosmetic vessels to today's colour palettes, makeup reflects society and personal identity. This overview highlights selected examples rather than a single universal chronology. Dates and interpretations can remain debated.

Explore a real Egyptian cosmetic tool ↗
CHAPTER 01 · Ancient Egypt · c. 3000 BCE onwards

Kohl, minerals and identity

Ancient Egyptian women and men used eye cosmetics, particularly dark mineral-based powders applied with small sticks. Surviving kohl vessels, mirrors and cosmetic kits provide extraordinary evidence. Today, historic mineral recipes should never be recreated on skin: some involved toxic lead compounds.

Evidence: The Metropolitan Museum of Art ↗
CHAPTER 02 · Mesopotamia and the ancient Mediterranean

Pigments, ceremony and status

Cosmetic preparation and adornment varied across Mesopotamia, Greece and Rome, spanning fragrant oils, face preparations and pigments. Sources often reflect elite practices rather than everyone's daily routine; regional variations and chronology matter.

Archaeological material and historical texts ↗
CHAPTER 03 · South Asia · many centuries

Kajal, bridal artistry and henna

Across India and neighbouring regions, kajal or surma, henna and ornamentation have diverse cultural histories. Elaborate bridal looks, eye definition and traditional adornment continue in many communities, with styles changing by region, religion and individual preference.

Living traditions, not a single fixed style ↗
CHAPTER 04 · China · dynastic histories

Powder, brows and lip colour

Chinese cosmetic traditions encompass face powders, shaped brows, lip colours and decorative forehead motifs in selected periods. Images of Tang-dynasty court fashion inspire modern interpretations, but reconstructions must be differentiated from evidence of everyday practice.

Styles differed by dynasty, class and setting ↗
CHAPTER 05 · Japan · Heian through modern

White face, brow traditions and theatrical codes

Heian elite beauty ideals included distinctive hair, face and brow practices; later kabuki and geisha-related makeup developed highly codified conventions. These are not interchangeable, nor do they represent how all Japanese people dressed. Contemporary J-beauty is a different subject.

Cultural context is essential ↗
CHAPTER 06 · Middle East and North Africa

Eye artistry across cultures

Kohl and related eye-decorating practices occur in distinct communities with different histories and meanings. Contemporary graphic liner, luminous skin and rich lipstick are modern creative choices rather than a uniform regional tradition.

Avoid copying ritual or ceremonial markings without context ↗
CHAPTER 07 · Europe · medieval to Renaissance

Pale ideals and dangerous recipes

Elite beauty fashions in parts of Europe at times prized a pale complexion. Some historical cosmetics relied on lead or mercury and carried serious health risks. Changes in morality, medicine, class and availability shaped how visible makeup could be.

Never reproduce historical hazardous ingredients ↗
CHAPTER 08 · 18th century · European courts

Powder, rouge and theatrical contrast

Powdered faces, bright rouge and beauty patches became fashionable in some aristocratic circles. Cosmetics expressed status, theatricality and the presentation of self; such styles were never universal across European society.

Court portraiture shows stylised ideals ↗
CHAPTER 09 · 19th century · beauty retail expands

New shops and changing respectability

Commercial cosmetics, new packaging and industrial manufacture gradually changed access. Makeup practices could remain discreet in some settings while theatre and performance embraced highly visible colour.

Commercial history differs from universal practice ↗
CHAPTER 10 · 1900–1930s · cinema transforms the face

Lashes, lipstick and the silver screen

Film and photography influenced the spread of defined brows, dark eye makeup and shaped lips. Early screen techniques were adapted to harsh lighting and monochrome cinematography, so they were not identical to everyday makeup.

Screen makeup is an art in its own right ↗
CHAPTER 11 · 1940–1960s · colour and consumer culture

Red lips, winged liner and new advertising

Fashion magazines, advertising and cinema popularised contrasting looks, from polished 1950s lips to 1960s graphic eyes. International adoption varied, and everyday looks were often simpler than famous editorials.

Popular imagery is not a whole society ↗
CHAPTER 12 · 1970–1990s · experimentation

Disco shine, blush, punk and supermodels

Shimmer, high-contrast eye colour, alternative music scenes, contouring and polished neutral looks all had their moments. Global media and subcultures cross-pollinated new techniques.

One decade contains multiple aesthetics ↗
CHAPTER 13 · 2000–2026 · beauty becomes global and digital

From tutorials to personal artistry

Online tutorials, creator communities and expanded shade ranges transformed learning and shopping. Skin-first makeup, expressive colour, K-beauty and J-beauty-inspired aesthetics, graphic liner and vintage revivals can coexist. Treat trends as options—not standards of beauty.

Individual preference, accessibility and safety first ↗

Historical caution: traditional kohl can contain hazardous metals; ancient whitening products sometimes contained lead or mercury. Do not reproduce these historical recipes. Use modern regulated cosmetics intended for the appropriate body area.

04 / THE STEP-BY-STEP MAKEUP ACADEMY

Every feature. Every step.

Practical instructions for skin, forehead, eyes, brows, lashes, nose, cheeks, lips, sculpting, finishing and hygiene. Start with a small amount of product and adapt the placement to your own features.

Full face · tutorial 01

Prep and skincare

Tools: Gentle cleanser, moisturiser, SPF, optional primer

  1. Wash hands and gently cleanse the face; pat dry without scrubbing.
  2. Use a moisturiser suited to your skin and let it absorb. Apply a broad-spectrum SPF 30+ in daytime independently of makeup.
  3. Apply primer only if needed; choose according to desired finish rather than assuming everyone needs it.
  4. Begin with small amounts of complexion product; build in thin layers to avoid pilling.

Professional note: A makeup product with incidental SPF is not a substitute for sufficient sunscreen.

Complexion · tutorial 02

Foundation

Tools: Foundation, complexion brush or sponge

  1. Match colour along the jaw in daylight and check the result after the formula settles.
  2. Start with a half-pump or small amount near the centre of the face.
  3. Blend outward using a clean brush, damp sponge or fingers; avoid making a heavy mask at the hairline.
  4. Press a thin second layer only where desired, leaving natural skin visible.
  5. Check the nose folds, jawline and forehead near daylight before setting.

Professional note: Deep skin tones may need multiple reference swatches; test actual undertone, not packaging.

Under-eyes and blemishes · tutorial 03

Concealer and correction

Tools: Concealer, small flat brush, clean fingers

  1. Choose a corrector or concealer shade close to your skin for blemishes; one slight step brighter may be used sparingly under eyes.
  2. Place tiny dots around the inner under-eye shadow rather than painting a thick triangle.
  3. Tap gently with a small brush or ring finger; keep product away from the lash line and waterline.
  4. For a spot, pat matching concealer precisely and wait briefly before blending the edges.
  5. Set with a small amount of powder only if creasing or shine is a concern.

Professional note: Peach/orange correctors can neutralise blue-grey darkness on some complexions; depth matters.

Shape and balance · tutorial 04

Forehead and temples

Tools: Soft powder brush, bronzer, optional contour

  1. Keep foundation thin near the hairline and blend into the temples.
  2. Place a little bronzer along the outer hairline if you want warmth, not to change your head's perceived size.
  3. Blend contour sparingly at the temples when desired, following your natural bone structure.
  4. Use a softly diffused highlight on the upper forehead only where you like its reflection.

Professional note: No face needs contour. Avoid placing a darker stripe in the centre of the forehead.

Eyebrows · tutorial 05

Brows

Tools: Spoolie, brow pencil or powder, optional gel

  1. Brush brow hairs upward or outward with a clean spoolie.
  2. Identify the sparse sections by looking straight into a mirror.
  3. Add fine hair-like strokes with a pencil or angled brush, applying light pressure.
  4. Blend through with the spoolie and set gently with clear or tinted brow gel.

Professional note: Do not over-pluck based on a single passing trend. Avoid getting product inside the eyes.

Eyelids and crease · tutorial 06

Eyeshadow

Tools: Eye-safe palette, fluffy blending and flat shader brushes

  1. Start with an eye-safe primer or very thin neutral base if it helps wear time.
  2. Sweep a light transition shade just above the natural fold; blend with small circular motions.
  3. Press a midtone onto the mobile lid, then a deeper shade to the outer third for dimension.
  4. Softly blend where colours meet; add shimmer to the centre if desired.
  5. Use a clean small brush for the lower lash line, avoiding loose glitter near the eye.

Professional note: Pressed pigments and craft glitter are not automatically approved for the immediate eye area. Check labels.

Upper lash line · tutorial 07

Eyeliner

Tools: Eye-safe pencil, gel or liquid liner, cotton bud

  1. Rest your elbow on a stable surface and look slightly down into a mirror.
  2. Draw tiny segments close to the upper lashes rather than one long sweeping stroke.
  3. For a wing, extend a short line following your preferred eye shape; connect it to the upper line.
  4. Refine with a fine brush or cotton bud. Let liquid liner dry before opening the eye fully.

Professional note: Do not apply eyeliner to irritated eyes. Waterline application is optional and can be uncomfortable.

Lashes · tutorial 08

Mascara

Tools: Mascara, optional clean lash comb

  1. Check that the wand and formula are clean and within the recommended use period.
  2. Position the brush near the upper lash roots without touching the eye surface.
  3. Move upward in small wiggles, then comb through ends with a light touch.
  4. For a second coat, apply while the first is still flexible if the formula allows.
  5. Remove later with a gentle suitable remover, without pulling lashes.

Professional note: Never share mascara or add water to a dried tube; discard contaminated products.

Nose · tutorial 09

Nose contour

Tools: Small contour brush, subtle matte contour

  1. Use a small amount of cool-neutral contour if you enjoy definition; skip it otherwise.
  2. Place two narrow, soft strokes along your own nose sides, keeping the shape symmetrical to your features.
  3. Diffuse completely with a small fluffy brush; harsh lines are more visible in daylight.
  4. If desired, use a touch of highlight on the bridge or tip, not both automatically.

Professional note: Contour is an artistic choice, not a corrective necessity; no nose shape needs fixing.

Cheeks · tutorial 10

Blush

Tools: Cream or powder blush, cheek brush or fingers

  1. Smile gently or feel for the upper cheek area, but apply based on your preferred lifted or rounded effect.
  2. For cream blush, place one tiny dot and tap outward before adding more.
  3. For powder blush, swirl once, tap off excess and sweep softly over cheeks.
  4. Blend toward the temples for lift or keep more central for a rounded effect.
  5. Check that blush meets the base seamlessly without a hard edge.

Professional note: Use a light hand—highly pigmented liquid blush can build quickly.

Cheekbones and jaw · tutorial 11

Bronzer, contour and highlight

Tools: Bronzer, cool-neutral contour, clean fluffy brushes, highlighter

  1. Bronzer adds warmth; contour imitates shadow—pick the purpose before choosing colour.
  2. Sweep bronzer where sunlight often naturally falls: outer forehead and upper cheeks.
  3. For contour, place a tiny amount beneath a cheekbone and blend upward rather than downward.
  4. Use highlighter on your preferred high points; avoid emphasising texture if that bothers you.
  5. Blend jaw and neck transitions in daylight.

Professional note: There is no universal diagonal stripe that flatters every face.

Lip outline and colour · tutorial 12

Lips

Tools: Lip balm, lip pencil, lipstick, optional gloss

  1. Exfoliation is optional; instead smooth dry lips with balm and blot the excess.
  2. Choose a liner close to the lipstick shade and trace the natural lip border with gentle strokes.
  3. Apply lipstick from the centre toward corners; blot and repeat for stronger colour.
  4. Add gloss to the middle for shine, or keep the finish matte or satin.
  5. Use a cotton bud to refine the edges and check teeth for transfer.

Professional note: Lip products should not be repurposed as eye makeup unless specifically eye-approved.

Final finish · tutorial 13

Setting and touch-ups

Tools: Blotting papers, powder puff, optional setting mist

  1. Inspect the full look in window light for unblended edges.
  2. Press a small puff of powder onto areas that tend to shine instead of powdering the entire face by default.
  3. If using setting spray, close eyes and mouth, follow the label and avoid inhaling the mist.
  4. For touch-ups, blot oils first, then reapply a minimal amount of makeup.
  5. Take a photograph in the lighting you will actually use if the look is intended for an event.

Professional note: Setting spray is not sunscreen, and aerosol products should be used in well-ventilated places.

After makeup · tutorial 14

Removal and tool hygiene

Tools: Remover, cleanser, soft cloth, brush cleanser

  1. Remove eye makeup gently with a suitable remover; do not scrub the eyelids.
  2. Cleanse the rest of the face thoroughly and moisturise afterward.
  3. Wash reusable sponges and brushes, then dry completely in a clean airy space.
  4. Replace eye cosmetics according to product guidance, and sooner following contamination or infection.
  5. Keep brushes and products personal rather than sharing them.

Professional note: Stop use if a product causes irritation; seek clinical advice for persistent reactions.

05 / COLOUR INTELLIGENCE

Find a palette that feels like you.

Makeup colour theory can help create harmony and contrast. It never needs to dictate what someone can or cannot wear based on complexion, age, gender or eye colour.

SHADE STORY

Soft neutrals

Daily versatility: beige, rose taupe, caramel and warm cocoa. Choose depth that shows against your skin.

SHADE STORY

Warm golden

Golden, terracotta and copper tones create a warm luminous direction.

SHADE STORY

Cool mauve

Dusty lavender, mauve and cool brown build understated definition.

SHADE STORY

Bright expressive

Fuchsia, teal, violet and gold work as a controlled accent or full editorial statement.

SHADE STORY

Lip wardrobe

Try warm red, blue-red, rose-nude and berry; formula and contrast matter as much as undertone.

SHADE STORY

Blush wardrobe

Peach, rosy pink, terracotta and wine tones can look natural across different skin depths in appropriate saturation.

SHADE STORY

Brow definition

Match depth and texture to brow hair; ashy or warm browns work differently across people.

SHADE STORY

Highlighter finishes

Gold, rose gold, champagne and pink pearl create distinct reflections.

HOW TO PICK A SHADE

Depth, undertone, contrast, finish.

Test foundation on your jaw rather than only your wrist. For blush and lipstick, consider saturation and how the colour reads against your individual skin depth. For eyes, try complementary contrast or a softer analogous palette. Lighting changes perception, so check your final look in daylight.

06 / THE COSMETIC ENCYCLOPAEDIA

Texture, formula and skin knowledge.

Understanding how products behave helps you buy fewer unnecessary things and use what you already own more effectively.

Finish

Matte reduces apparent shine; satin balances glow and refinement; dewy reflects more light; metallic and glitter finishes create intentional sparkle.

Coverage

Sheer evens subtly; medium covers more; full is opaque. Build thin coats rather than choosing the heaviest formula by default.

Formula

Liquid and cream textures can feel flexible; powder can control shine; sticks are portable. Dry and oily zones may need different strategies.

Undertone

Warm, cool, neutral and olive are descriptive starting points, not strict rules. Test in daylight and allow for oxidation or colour shifts.

Skin depth

Fair, light, medium, tan, deep and very deep are broad merchandising labels. Always compare actual shades on your own skin.

Eye shape

Hooded, monolid, deep-set, round and almond-shaped are useful descriptive terms, but every eye is unique; place colour while looking straight ahead.

Colour theory

Complementary contrast can intensify a look; analogous tones are harmonious. Choose based on desired effect, not rules for eye colour.

Ingredients

Pigments, binders, waxes, oils, film formers, preservatives and silicones contribute to texture, colour and wear. Read the current INCI list.

SPF and makeup

Sunscreen is applied as a separate adequate layer; a small amount of foundation with SPF rarely provides the labelled protection.

Shelf life

The open-jar PAO symbol shows how many months many products are intended to last after opening. Eye-area products may require more frequent replacement.

Texture and mature skin

Thin, flexible layers often settle more naturally than heavy powder. There are no age restrictions on shimmer, red lipstick or colour.

Sensitive skin

Fragrance-free may be preferable for some. Patch-test when appropriate, check allergens, and avoid applying products to inflamed or injured skin.

08 / THE MAKEUP TOOLBOX

Sixteen accessories explained.

Good tools make application easier, but you do not need every brush on the market. Build around the products you actually use, and keep every applicator clean.

🖌️

Foundation brush

Dense synthetic bristles for controlled liquid application. Wash regularly and dry flat or with bristles downward.

Typical shopping budget: €8–30

🫧

Blending sponge

Dampen, squeeze out excess water and bounce onto skin; never rub aggressively. Wash and dry after use.

Typical shopping budget: €5–25

🎨

Concealer detail brush

Small rounded or flat head for precise spot and under-eye blending.

Typical shopping budget: €4–20

☁️

Powder brush and puff

Large fluffy brush for a light veil; compact puff for targeted setting and touch-ups.

Typical shopping budget: €5–25

🌷

Blush brush

Soft medium-sized brush for diffuse powder application; cream blush can also be tapped with fingers.

Typical shopping budget: €6–25

🌙

Contour brush

Small angled brush to place subtle shadow; use a clean fluffy brush to soften edges.

Typical shopping budget: €7–28

✨

Highlighter fan or detail brush

Small soft brush offers greater control than a huge sweep of shimmer.

Typical shopping budget: €5–20

👁️

Flat eyeshadow brush

Packs colour on the lid, especially pressed mattes and shimmers.

Typical shopping budget: €4–19

🌀

Fluffy eye blending brush

Diffuses borders between eye colours without removing all pigment.

Typical shopping budget: €4–22

✒️

Angled liner/brow brush

Precise tip for gel eyeliner or brow powder; keep separate from the waterline.

Typical shopping budget: €4–18

🪮

Spoolie and lash comb

Neatens brow hairs and can separate lashes carefully after product application.

Typical shopping budget: €2–13

10 / BEAUTY THAT CARES FOR YOU

Safe makeup is beautiful makeup.

Learn practical habits for hygiene, sunscreen, expiration dates and ingredient awareness. These guidelines are educational and not a substitute for professional advice.

Eye-area hygiene

Use only eye-approved makeup around the eyes, never share mascara or eye liners, and never add water to old formulas. Remove makeup gently and discard products after an eye infection.

Skin and allergies

Read labels, check known allergens and avoid using cosmetics over broken or inflamed skin. “Natural” is not a safety guarantee. Stop immediately if irritation occurs.

Safe colour and shimmer

Check eye-area warnings on pigments and glitter. Do not use craft glitter or decorative pigments not approved for cosmetic eye use.

Product expiration

Look for the open-jar symbol and follow label instructions. Replace mascara promptly after contamination; many manufacturers recommend a short life after opening.

Sun protection

Use separate, adequate broad-spectrum SPF 30+ during daytime; an SPF-labelled foundation is not usually applied in sufficient quantity to be the only protection.

Brush hygiene

Wash reusable tools frequently with suitable mild cleanser and let them dry fully; keep personal-use products away from shared testers and dirty storage.

Eye infections, persistent irritation or an allergic reaction deserve professional assessment. Stop the relevant product, do not cover inflamed skin with more makeup, and consult the product label or a healthcare professional as appropriate.

11 / ANSWERS WITHOUT BEAUTY MYTHS

Your makeup questions, answered.

Practical answers to common questions. Open a topic to see the full guidance.

Must I use primer?

No. If moisturiser and your foundation already work well together, primer may be unnecessary.

Should I apply cream products before powder?

Cream and liquid products are often easier to blend before a powder set, but modern formulas vary; lightly powder only where needed.

How do I find a foundation shade?

Compare several shades along the jaw in daylight, let them settle, and check both depth and undertone on your actual skin.

What if eyeshadow falls beneath my eyes?

Brush away loose powder gently before adding concealer; using less shadow and pressing rather than sweeping can reduce fallout.

Can I use lipstick as blush or eyeshadow?

Only where the specific product is labelled for that use. Lip products are not automatically safe for eyes, and bright pigments may have area restrictions.

What makeup is best for beginners?

Start with a small, manageable kit: tinted base or concealer, blush, mascara, brow product, one versatile eyeshadow and lip product.

Should I contour my nose?

Only if you enjoy the artistic effect. It is not necessary and is never a requirement for a particular nose shape.

How often should I wash brushes?

Wash regularly, especially tools used with liquid products, and dry them completely. Frequency depends on use and your hygiene needs.

Can makeup replace sunscreen?

No. The amount of foundation normally worn rarely reaches the labelled sunscreen dose. Use separate broad-spectrum SPF 30+.

What if mascara irritates my eyes?

Stop using it. Avoid eye makeup during inflammation or infection; consult a clinician if symptoms persist.

Are high-priced products always better?

No. Shade match, suitability, technique and comfort matter more than price or brand prestige.

What is the best makeup for older skin?

There is no universal age-specific rule. Thin, flexible textures and well-prepared skin can be comfortable at any age; use the finishes you like.
